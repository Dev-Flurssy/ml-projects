HOW A MODEL INGEST DATA WITH FEATURE VECTOR

A machine‑learning model does not understand what a car, apartment, or patient is.  
It only understands numbers.  
When a record enters the model, it is immediately turned into a feature vector — a list of numeric values arranged in a fixed order.  
This means the model never sees the real object, only the numbers that describe it.

------------------------------------------------------------  
FROM A RECORD TO A VECTOR

Take one car from the dataset:

- 4 years old  
- 56,000 km  
- 1,600 cc engine  
- Sold for 11,200 GBP  

The model converts this into:

[4, 56000, 1600, 11200]

Each number sits in a fixed position that always represents the same feature.  
The model reads the vector from left to right and does not know what the numbers mean beyond their numeric value.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHY THIS MATTERS

- Order matters: every row must follow the same feature order  
- Everything must be numeric: models only work with numbers  
- Every row must have the same length: all records produce vectors of identical shape  
- Stacking all vectors forms a matrix the model uses during training  

The model treats each feature vector as a point in space.  
Similar cars appear close together, unusual cars appear far away.

------------------------------------------------------------  
MULTICOLLINEARITY MEANS

Multicollinearity means two or more numerical features in your dataset carry almost the same information.
They move together, rise together, fall together, and behave so similarly that the model cannot tell which one is actually responsible for the effect.

When features are highly correlated, the model sees them as duplicates rather than independent signals.
This makes the model unstable, inflates variance, and causes unreliable or overly sensitive weight estimates.

------------------------------------------------------------  
THE COMPLETE NUMERICAL FEATURE INSPECTION CHECKLIST  


1 RANGE  
What it tells you:  
- Are values tiny or huge  
- Are scales very different across features  
- Will gradient descent struggle  

Why it matters:  
A feature ranging from 0.001 to 1,000,000 will dominate others unless scaled.

------------------------------------------------------------  
2 SHAPE (DISTRIBUTION)  
What it tells you:  
- Symmetric  
- Left‑skewed  
- Right‑skewed  
- Bimodal  

Why it matters:  
Skewed data often needs a log transform to stabilize variance and reduce extreme values.

------------------------------------------------------------  
3 OUTLIERS  
What it tells you:  
- Are there extreme values far from the rest  
- Are they errors or rare but real events  

Why it matters:  
Outliers distort scaling, means, and gradients.  
The 1.5 × IQR rule is commonly used to detect them.

------------------------------------------------------------  
4 SENTINEL VALUES  
What it tells you:  
- Are there magic numbers like −1, 0, or 999 meaning “unknown”  

Why it matters:  
Models treat these as real measurements unless you fix them.

------------------------------------------------------------   
5 MISSINGNESS PATTERNS  
What it tells you:  
- How many values are missing  
- Whether missingness is random or patterned  

Why it matters:  
Non‑random missingness can signal bias or hidden structure.

------------------------------------------------------------  
6 CONSTANT OR NEAR‑CONSTANT FEATURES  
What it tells you:  
- Does the feature have almost no variation  

Why it matters:  
It carries no useful information and can be removed.

------------------------------------------------------------  
7 CORRELATIONS BETWEEN FEATURES  
What it tells you:  
- Are two features basically the same  
- Is there multicollinearity  

Why it matters:  
Redundant features confuse linear models and inflate variance.

------------------------------------------------------------  
8 CLUSTERS OR NATURAL GROUPS  
What it tells you:  
- Does the distribution have multiple peaks  

Why it matters:  
Suggests segmentation or binning.

------------------------------------------------------------  
9 SEASONALITY OR TIME PATTERNS  
What it tells you:  
- Do values change by hour, day, or month  

Why it matters:  
Time‑based features may be needed.

------------------------------------------------------------  
10 DRIFT OVER TIME  
What it tells you:  
- Are values shifting across months or years  

Why it matters:  
Models trained on old distributions fail on new ones.

------------------------------------------------------------  
11 SPARSITY  
What it tells you:  
- Are there many zeros  
- Are non‑zero values rare  

Why it matters:  
Suggests binarization or special handling.

------------------------------------------------------------  
12 NON‑LINEAR RELATIONSHIPS  
What it tells you:  
- Does the feature relate to the target in a curved or threshold pattern  

Why it matters:  
Linear models may fail; tree‑based models or feature engineering may be needed.

------------------------------------------------------------  
13 DUPLICATE ROWS OR REPEATED PATTERNS  
What it tells you:  
- Are there repeated entries  

Why it matters:  
Duplicates distort the true distribution and bias the model.

------------------------------------------------------------  
14 FEATURE INTERACTIONS  
What it tells you:  
- Do two features together reveal something important  

Why it matters:  
Interactions often matter more than individual features.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHAT THE NUMBERS TELL YOU

The minimum and maximum values show the raw range of each feature.  
Imagine a dataset of cars:

- mileage ranges from 500 km to 200,000 km  
- age ranges from 1 year to 12 years  

Mileage spans a huge range compared to age.  
This means mileage is on a much larger scale.  
If you feed these raw numbers into gradient descent, the model will pay far more attention to mileage simply because the numbers are bigger.  
This is why scaling or normalization is needed.

------------------------------------------------------------  
MEAN VS MEDIAN 

Suppose you have a feature called “paid price” with these values:

[5,000, 5,200, 5,300, 5,400, 50,000]

Mean:  
(5000 + 5200 + 5300 + 5400 + 50000) / 5 = 13,780  

Median:  
5,300  

Here the mean is much higher than the median.  
This tells you the distribution is right‑skewed.  
One very large value (50,000) pulls the mean upward.  
A log transform would compress the large value and make the distribution more balanced.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHAT IF THE MEDIAN IS HIGHER THAN THE MEAN?

This means the distribution is left‑skewed.

Example:

[100, 120, 130, 140, 5]

Mean:  
(100 + 120 + 130 + 140 + 5) / 5 = 99  

Median:  
120  

Here the median (120) is higher than the mean (99).  
This happens because one very small value (5) pulls the mean downward.  
A left‑skewed distribution often suggests:

- many values are clustered at the high end  
- a few very small values drag the average down  
- you may need to investigate whether the small values are errors or sentinel values  
- sometimes a square transform or shifting the data helps, depending on the context  

------------------------------------------------------------  
OUTLIERS 

Using the 1.5 × IQR rule:

Suppose Q1 = 5,000 and Q3 = 5,400.  
IQR = 400.  
1.5 × IQR = 600.

Lower bound = Q1 − 600 = 4,400  
Upper bound = Q3 + 600 = 6,000  

Any value below 4,400 or above 6,000 is an outlier.

So in the earlier example, 50,000 is an outlier.  
This outlier could be:

- a genuine rare case  
- a data‑entry mistake  
- a sentinel value meaning “unknown”  

Each type requires a different cleaning approach.

------------------------------------------------------------  
NORMALIZATION

When features are raw, their scales can be extremely different.  
In the car dataset:

- age ranges from 1 to 12  
- mileage ranges from 500 to 200000  

A model only sees numbers. Because mileage values are so much larger, they dominate distance calculations.  
For example, in k‑nearest neighbours:

- a difference of 1 year in age  
- versus a difference of 1000 km in mileage  

The model treats the mileage difference as far more important, even if age is actually a strong predictor.  
This is why age gets ignored.

Gradient descent also struggles.  
The loss surface becomes a long, narrow valley.  
Instead of moving smoothly toward the minimum, the algorithm zig‑zags slowly because one feature has a huge scale compared to the other.

Normalization fixes this by rescaling all features to a common range before training.

------------------------------------------------------------  
MIN‑MAX SCALING — SIMPLE PRACTICAL EXAMPLE

Formula:  
x' = (x − min) / (max − min)

Suppose mileage ranges from 500 to 200000.  
Take a car with mileage = 50000.

x' = (50000 − 500) / (200000 − 500)  
x' = 49500 / 199500  
x' ≈ 0.248

Now mileage is a number between 0 and 1.  
Age will also be scaled to between 0 and 1.  
This puts both features on equal footing.

Weakness:  
If one car has mileage = 900000, the max becomes 900000.  
All other cars get squeezed into a tiny part of the range.  
The model loses meaningful variation.

------------------------------------------------------------  
Z‑SCORE STANDARDISATION — SIMPLE PRACTICAL EXAMPLE

Formula:  
x' = (x − mean) / std_dev

Suppose mileage has:

- mean = 60000  
- std_dev = 30000  

Take a car with mileage = 50000.

x' = (50000 − 60000) / 30000  
x' = −10000 / 30000  
x' = −0.33

This means the car’s mileage is slightly below average.  
The transformed feature has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

Why this is better:  
Outliers do not compress the entire range because the denominator is the spread (std dev), not the full min‑max range.  
This is why Z‑score is the more common default.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHY YOU MUST COMPUTE SCALING ON TRAINING DATA ONLY

You must compute min, max, mean, and std dev only on the training set.  
Then apply those same saved numbers to validation and test sets.

Practical example:

Training mileage values:  
[10000, 20000, 30000, 40000]

mean = 25000  
std_dev = 11180

Test mileage value:  
50000

You compute:  
(50000 − 25000) / 11180  
= 2.24

If you recompute mean and std dev using the test value, you leak information from the test set into training.  
This makes evaluation unrealistically good and breaks the ML workflow.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHY SCALING SPEEDS UP GRADIENT DESCENT

When two features have very different scales, the loss surface becomes a long, narrow valley.  
Imagine one feature ranges from 1 to 12, and another ranges from 500 to 200000.  
Gradient descent tries to move downhill, but the valley is stretched out.  
It must take many small zig‑zag steps across the narrow dimension before it can move forward along the long dimension.  
This makes training slow and inefficient.

After scaling, both features have similar ranges.  
The valley becomes more circular instead of elongated.  
Gradient descent can move directly toward the minimum in far fewer steps.  
Training becomes faster and more stable.

------------------------------------------------------------  
EXAMPLE

Suppose you have two features:

Feature A (age): 1 to 12  
Feature B (mileage): 500 to 200000  

Raw scales:

- Age difference between two cars: 3 years  
- Mileage difference between two cars: 30000 km  

Gradient descent sees:

3 vs 30000  

The large feature dominates the shape of the loss surface.  
The model spends most of its time adjusting weights for mileage and barely moves in the direction of age.

After scaling (for example, Z‑score):

Age might become values like −0.5, 0.2, 1.1  
Mileage might become values like −0.3, 0.4, 1.7  

Now both features have similar magnitudes.  
The loss surface becomes balanced, and gradient descent moves smoothly toward the minimum.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHEN NOT TO SCALE

Tree‑based models (decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting) do not care about the magnitude of features.  
They only care about ordering:

Is value A less than value B?  
Is value B greater than value C?

Because they split based on thresholds, not distances, scaling brings no benefit.  
It does not harm them, but it does not help either.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHEN YOU SHOULD SCALE

For almost every other model family, scaling is important:

- linear models  
- logistic regression  
- neural networks  
- support vector machines  
- k‑nearest neighbours  

These models rely on distances, gradients, or geometric relationships.  
Uneven feature scales distort these relationships, slow training, and reduce accuracy.  
Scaling fixes this.

------------------------------------------------------------  

WHY BINNING IS NEEDED

Some numerical features do not follow a straight, smooth relationship with the label.  
A linear model can only learn straight‑line patterns.  
If the true pattern is curved, changes direction, or has multiple phases, scaling alone cannot fix it.  
Binning is used when the feature behaves in a way a straight line cannot capture.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHAT BINNING DOES

Binning replaces a continuous numerical value with a categorical bucket.  
Each bucket represents a range of values.  
The model learns a separate weight for each bucket.  
This allows a linear model to approximate curved or irregular patterns by treating each region of the feature’s range independently.

------------------------------------------------------------  
PRACTICAL EXAMPLE: CAR RESALE VALUE

Consider the relationship between a car’s age and its resale value.  
This relationship is not a straight line:

- very new cars keep value  
- mid‑age cars drop sharply  
- very old cars may stabilize or even increase in value  

A linear model cannot capture a pattern that goes flat → steep drop → flat again.  
Binning solves this by dividing age into groups such as:

- new  
- mid‑age  
- older  
- classic  

Each group gets its own weight.  
This turns a curved pattern into a step pattern the model can learn.

------------------------------------------------------------  
ONE‑HOT ENCODING THE BINS

After binning, each bin is converted into its own binary column.  
If a car belongs to bin 3, that column is 1 and the others are 0.  
The model learns an independent coefficient for each bin.  
This gives the model flexibility to approximate curves without assuming the bins follow a numeric order.

Keeping the bin index as a single integer implies an ordering the model may not respect, so one‑hot encoding is preferred.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WHEN TO BIN

Binning is most useful when:

1. the relationship between the feature and the label is non‑monotonic  
2. the feature has many outliers you want to cap  
3. your model is linear and you want it to approximate a curve  

Tree‑based models already find splits automatically, so explicit binning adds little value for them.

------------------------------------------------------------  
DATA SCRUBBING

Data scrubbing, also called data cleaning, is the work of fixing the problems that appear between the real world and your training table.  
Models cannot repair corrupted input on their own.  
The four most common problems are missing values, duplicate rows, sentinel values, and outliers.  
Each must be handled deliberately before training.

------------------------------------------------------------  
THE FOUR PROBLEMS AND THEIR USUAL REMEDIES

Missing values  
What it looks like: NaN, blank, null  
Common fix: Fill with the training median for numeric features or the mode for categorical features.  
If only a few rows are affected, you can drop them.

Duplicates  
What it looks like: identical rows  
Common fix: Keep one copy.  
Dropping all copies removes real information, so only remove the extra duplicates.

Sentinel values  
What it looks like: special numbers such as −1, 0, or 999 used to mean “unknown”  
Common fix: Replace with the feature’s median, or add a separate binary column indicating that the value was missing.  
This prevents the model from treating the sentinel as a real measurement.

Outliers  
What it looks like: values many standard deviations away from the mean  
Common fix: Clip at a sensible cap, use a robust loss such as MAE that tolerates outliers, or investigate manually 
to determine whether the value is real or an error.

------------------------------------------------------------  
EXPLANATION

Missing values  
Example: A car’s mileage is blank.  
If most cars have mileage around 60,000, you fill the blank with the median mileage.  
This keeps the feature consistent without introducing extreme values.

Duplicates  
Example: The same car appears twice in the dataset.  
You keep one copy because it represents a real observation.  
Removing both copies would erase information.

Sentinel values  
Example: Age = −1 meaning “unknown”.  
The model does not know −1 means missing; it treats it as a real age.  
Replacing −1 with the median age or adding a “was missing” indicator prevents confusion.

Outliers  
Example: Mileage = 900,000 when most cars are between 10,000 and 200,000.  
You can clip the value to a maximum such as 250,000, or use a robust loss that reduces the impact of extreme points.  
If the value looks suspicious, you investigate manually.

------------------------------------------------------------  
FEATURE QUALITY EXPLANATION

Not every number in a database should be handed to a model.  
A good numerical feature has specific qualities that make it meaningful, stable, and useful.  
Getting these qualities right often improves model performance more than changing the model architecture.

The table below shows examples of good and risky features:

Property | Good example | Risky example  
Clear meaning | mileage: km driven since new | combined_score: proprietary aggregate  
No sentinel values | null for unknown (handled separately) | −1 for unknown mileage  
Bounded, plausible range | age: 0 to 30 years | price: can be negative in some exports  
Enough variation | mileage varies widely across cars | colour_code: 95% of rows are code 1  
Stable over time | engine size: rarely changes | feature whose distribution drifts  

------------------------------------------------------------  
CLEAR MEANING

A feature must have a simple, singular meaning.  
If you cannot explain in one sentence what the number measures, the model will struggle to use it.  
Aggregated scores, ratios of ratios, or IDs disguised as numeric values often confuse the model.  
A clear feature is directly tied to a physical or measurable quantity.

Example:  
mileage = kilometres driven since new  
This is clear and interpretable.

Risky example:  
combined_score = proprietary aggregate  
The model cannot learn reliably from a number whose meaning is unclear or inconsistent.

------------------------------------------------------------  
REASONABLE, BOUNDED RANGE

A feature should have a physically plausible range.  
If a value can be negative or extremely large due to data errors, the model will learn incorrect patterns.  
Define the acceptable range in advance and flag anything outside it.

Example:  
age = 0 to 30 years  
This is realistic.

Risky example:  
price = negative values due to export errors  
Negative prices are not physically meaningful and indicate data‑quality problems.

------------------------------------------------------------  
NO SENTINEL VALUES

Sentinel values such as −1, 0, or 999 used to mean “unknown” poison the feature.  
The model treats them as real measurements and learns patterns based on missingness rather than actual values.

Correct approach:  
Use null for unknown and handle missingness separately.

Risky example:  
mileage = −1 meaning “unknown”  
The model learns that −1 predicts something, but only because those rows are missing data.

------------------------------------------------------------  
SUFFICIENT VARIATION

A feature must vary enough to carry information.  
If 95% of rows have the same value, the feature contributes almost nothing.  
It may still be useful as a rare indicator, but treat it as a binary flag rather than a continuous measurement.

Example:  
mileage varies widely across cars  
This provides useful signal.

Risky example:  
colour_code = 1 for 95% of rows  
This is effectively a constant and should be treated as a binary indicator.

------------------------------------------------------------  
STABLE DISTRIBUTION BETWEEN TRAINING AND SERVING

A feature must remain stable over time.  
If the distribution shifts after deployment because the data source changes or the population changes, the model’s learned weights become mismatched to reality.  
Monitoring feature distributions in production is essential.

Example:  
engine size rarely changes in the dataset  
This is stable.

Risky example:  
a feature whose distribution drifts significantly over time  
This causes silent degradation even if loss metrics look normal.

------------------------------------------------------------  
THE SIMPLEST SCREEN

Look at the feature’s histogram on training data and on a recent production sample side‑by‑side.  
If the shapes differ noticeably, the feature may be causing silent degradation and should be reviewed.

------------------------------------------------------------  
FEATURE QUALITY CHECKLIST

Rate a hypothetical feature:  
“number of days since the car’s last service, where −1 means the service history is unknown.”

Check each criterion:

Has clear, singular meaning  
No sentinel values used for missing data  
Bounded, physically plausible range  
Sufficient variation  
Likely stable between training and deployment

POLYNOMIAL FEATURES 

A linear model predicts:

y' = b + w x

This is always a straight line.  
If the true relationship between x and y is curved, no choice of w and b will make the line fit well.  
The model will underfit because a straight line cannot follow a curved pattern.

Polynomial features fix this by adding powers of x:

y' = b + w1 x + w2 x² + w3 x³ + …

The model is still linear in its weights, but because the inputs include x², x³, and higher powers, the output can form curves.  
This is feature engineering: you create new features (x², x³, …) from an existing one (x) and add them to the design matrix.  
The model itself remains a simple linear learner.

------------------------------------------------------------  
PRACTICAL EXAMPLE

Suppose the true pattern between age and price looks like a U‑shape:

- very new items are expensive  
- mid‑age items are cheaper  
- very old items become expensive again  

A straight line cannot capture this.  
But a polynomial model with x and x² can bend into a U‑shape.  
The model learns weights for x and x² that together produce a curve.

------------------------------------------------------------  
SCALING MATTERS

Polynomial features grow extremely fast:

- if x is in thousands  
- x² is in millions  
- x³ is in billions  

This creates huge differences in magnitude across columns.  
Gradient descent becomes unstable and slow.

Always scale x before forming polynomial features.  
Use Z‑score or min‑max scaling so that x, x², and x³ are on similar ranges.

------------------------------------------------------------  
WATCH OUT FOR HIGH DEGREES

High‑degree polynomials fit the training data extremely well but generalise poorly.  
They create wiggly curves that follow noise instead of signal.

Degrees 2 and 3 cover most practical curved relationships.  
If degree 3 still fits poorly, reconsider whether:

- the feature is the right one to transform  
- a different feature captures the pattern better  
- the relationship is not polynomial at all
